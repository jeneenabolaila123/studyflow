1. Researching Background Information
● RESEARCH is essential before drafting a message:
○ Research involves collecting information about a speciﬁc topic
● It helps shape the message and build credibility.
● Business writers collect information to answer several questions:
○ What does the receiver need to know?
○ What is the receiver supposed to do?
○ How is the receiver supposed to do it?
○ When must the receiver do it?
○ What are the consequences if the receiver doesn't do it?
● Research can be informal or formal, depending on the message 
purpose.

2. Informal Research Methods
● Search company ﬁles: Consult your own ﬁles, company wiki, and other 
digital/manual ﬁles for background information.
● Talk with your manager: Gather information and guidance from your 
manager, who may have knowledge and suggestions on the topic.
● Interview the target audience: Engage individuals from the intended 
audience to gain insights on their needs and preferences.
● Conduct an informal survey: Utilize questionnaires, telephone surveys, or 
online surveys to gather helpful but unscientiﬁc information.
● Brainstorm for ideas: Engage in idea generation sessions alone or in 
small groups, recording at least a dozen ideas without judgment.

3. Formal Research Methods
Complex business problems require formal research methods:
● Access electronic sources: digital catalogues, Google search
● Search manually: public and college libraries, books, articles, 
encyclopedias, reference books
● Investigate primary sources: visit locations, conduct traffic counts, search 
blogs and social media, use questionnaires, interviews, or focus groups
● Conduct scientiﬁc experiments: present choices with controlled variables
Examples of research topics for managerial decision making (related to 
clothing):
○ Consumer preferences between Roots and H&M sweatpants
○ Factors inﬂuencing consumers' purchase of sweaters, jackets, and cold-weather 
gear

Organizing Information to Show Relationships
● Organizing involves GROUPING and STRATEGIZING.
● Well-organized messages:
○ group similar items together and follow a logical sequence
○ Do not lack emphasis and do not confuse readers
● Two techniques for organizing data: scratch list and outline.
○ Scratch list → making a list of topics and writing the message from it
○ Outlines → organize complex ideas and provide clarity

Composing Drafts With Eﬀective Sentences
● Organize ideas and create an outline to overcome writer's block
● Minimize interruptions to maintain focus
● Consider your preferred writing style:
○ Freewriting and deliberate thinking are both valid approaches
● Write quickly to capture initial thoughts and reﬁne them in later versions
● Imagine speaking to the reader as you start each idea
● Use substitute words or placeholders if you can't think of the right word at 
once
➔ Remember: The ﬁrst draft is NOT the ﬁnal version; revisions and 
polishing can be done later.

● Fragments are broken-off parts of complex sentences.
● Punctuating fragments as complete sentences is a serious error.
● Fragments can be identiﬁed by words such as although, as, because, even, 
except, for example, if, instead of, since, such as, that, which, and when.
● Add words to the sentence or remove words to ﬁx fragments.

Run-on 
(fused) 
sentences
● Two independent clauses in a sentence must be joined by a 
coordinating conjunction (and, or, nor, but) or a semicolon (;)
● Run-on sentence occurs when there is no conjunction or semicolon
● Run-on sentences should be avoided in writing

[Page 19]
Comma- 
splice 
sentences
● Comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined 
by a comma only.
● Independent clauses can be joined by coordinating 
conjunctions or conjunctive adverbs. Coordinating conjunctions 
require only a comma.
● Conjunctive adverbs require a semicolon and a comma.

1. Show Emphasis
IN CONVERSATION
● Emphasizing main ideas by speaking loudly or repeating them slowly
● Pounding the table for real emphasis
● Raising eyebrows, shaking the head, or whispering in a low voice
IN WRITING
● Using mechanics such as:
○ Underlining
○ Italics and boldface
○ Font changes (size, style)
○ ALL CAPS
○ Dashes – - (used sparingly)

TIPS on structure
● Place the important idea ﬁrst or last. 
● Ideas have less competition from surrounding words when they appear ﬁrst 
or last in a sentence.
● Give the important idea the spotlight.
● Don’t dilute the effect of the main idea by making it share the stage with 
other words and clauses.
➔ Instead, put the main idea, separately, in a simple sentence or in 
an independent clause.

2. Use the Active and Passive Voice Eﬀectively
ACTIVE VOICE
● The subject performs the action.
○ is more direct, revealing the performer immediately
○ is easier to understand and usually shorter
● Most business writing should be in the active voice.
PASSIVE VOICE
● The subject receives the action.
○ useful for emphasizing actions over people
○ can de-emphasize negative news
○ can conceal the doer of an action

Parallelism in structure creates balanced writing.
● Parallel sentences are easy to read and understand.
● Avoid disturbing the reader's expectations by maintaining 
parallelism in lists
● Match nouns with nouns, verbs with verbs, and clauses with clauses
● Avoid mixing active-voice verbs with passive-voice verbs.
● Use similar structures to express similar ideas for parallelism.
○ Examples of parallel words: computing, coding, recording, storing

● Craft a Topic Sentence: A uniﬁed paragraph develops a single main idea.
○ Example: "This report investigates consumer conﬁdence changes in Canada in the 
past 10 years."
● Develop Support Sentences: Add sentences related to the topic sentence to 
discuss and explain details.
● Build Paragraph Coherence: Coherent paragraphs are linked ideas that 
lead logically from one to the next, taking readers through a series of steps.
● Techniques for helping readers follow the writer's ideas include:
○ Repeating a key idea using the same or similar expression.
○ Using pronouns to refer to previous nouns.
○ Showing connections with transitional expressions like: consequently, 
however, as a result, and meanwhile.
